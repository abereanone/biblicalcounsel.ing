Ask what the method says the problem is, what causes it, what the goal is, what brings change, and who gets the credit for change. If Scripture's answers to those questions are replaced by someone else's, the method is not biblical, even if it quotes the Bible.

## Proofs
- 1 Thessalonians 5:21
- Colossians 2:8
- Isaiah 8:20
- 2 Timothy 3:16-17
- 1 Corinthians 3:7

"Test all things. Hold fast to what is good" (1 Thessalonians 5:21). Christians are offered many counseling methods, some secular, some Christian, some a mixture. A method can borrow Christian words and still work from an unbiblical view of the person.

Pastor and biblical counselor Matthew Statler suggests five questions to put to any method:

1. **What defines the problem?** Is it named in Scripture's categories, such as sin, suffering, unbelief, fear or idolatry, or only in the method's own?
2. **What explains it?** Does the method trace the trouble to the heart before God, or only to the body, the past, or the environment?
3. **What is the goal?** Christlikeness and the glory of God, or calm, function and self-fulfillment?
4. **What are the means?** The Word, prayer, faith, repentance and the church, or techniques that could work without God?
5. **To whom is change attributed?** God working by His Spirit, or the technique, the therapist or the client?

The questions help especially with fashionable approaches. Some trauma therapies, for example, make the body's sensations the main object of attention, or treat contested theories as settled science. Bodily reactions are real, and medical facts are welcome. But when a method claims to explain why a person responds as he does and how he will be changed, it is speaking where Scripture has already spoken, and Scripture must have the last word. "Neither he who plants nor he who waters is anything, but only God, who makes things grow" (1 Corinthians 3:7).
